\section{Introduction} \label{sec-intro}

The Fiat--Shamir transform~\cite{C:FiaSha86} turns a three-move identification protocol into a signature scheme by computing the verifier's challenge as a hash of the first message and the signed message. Applied to the Schnorr protocol it gives Schnorr signatures~\cite{C:Schnorr89}, among the most widely used pieces of cryptography today, typically in the form of EdDSA~\cite{eddsa}, deployed in TLS and SSH and, over secp256k1, in Bitcoin since the Taproot upgrade~\cite{add:BIP340}. Applied to a lattice protocol with rejection sampling it gives Dilithium~\cite{Dilithium}, standardized by NIST in 2024 as ML-DSA in FIPS~204~\cite{FIPS204}. In both cases the security analyses that support deployment model the hash function as a random oracle~\cite{CCS:BelRog93}, sometimes with further idealizations of the group~\cite{EC:FucPloSeu20,JMC:NevSmaWar09,EPRINT:Shoup23,C:CLMQ21}. In reality the hash function is fixed and public, and its code is available to everyone. We ask what survives when the random oracle is \emph{instantiated}, that is, replaced by an actual hash function. By an instantiation we mean an efficiently evaluable keyed hash family, generated honestly and published in the verification key, whose key may be generated together with the signing key and may depend on the verification key. Two of our results also modify the signature scheme, by adding a salt and a pre-hash, and we say so where they appear.
\begin{quote}
\emph{Can deployed Fiat--Shamir signature schemes be proved secure in the standard model, and under what assumptions?}
\end{quote}

The question is known to be hard, and the two lines of work that address it leave exactly these schemes untouched. On the negative side, Fiat--Shamir is uninstantiable in general: Goldwasser and Kalai~\cite{FOCS:GolKal03} construct protocols for which the transform is insecure with \emph{every} concrete hash function. On the positive side, correlation-intractable hashing~\cite{JACM:CanGolHal04} instantiates Fiat--Shamir for statistically sound interactive proofs, and such hash functions are now known from standard assumptions~\cite{EC:CCRR18,STOC:CCHLRRW19,C:PeiShi19}. That route does not reach Schnorr or Dilithium. Correlation intractability rules out a prover finding a first message whose challenge is \emph{bad}, meaning one that would let a false statement be accepted. No false statements arise in the relations underlying these two schemes. Every group element has a discrete logarithm, and every honestly generated Dilithium verification key has a short secret. Soundness is therefore vacuous, and there is no bad-challenge relation for correlation intractability to act on. What a signature scheme needs instead is unforgeability, and the random-oracle proofs obtain it by extracting from the forger, either by programming the oracle or by rewinding it. Neither operation is available for a fixed hash function.

So the instantiability of the schemes people actually use is unresolved. For Schnorr, essentially one standard-model result exists: Paillier and Vergnaud~\cite{AC:PaiVer05} showed that for \emph{every} hash function, Schnorr signatures resist \emph{key recovery} under chosen-message attack, a notion they call BK-CMA, assuming the one-more discrete-logarithm (OMDL) problem~\cite{JC:BNPS03} is hard. OMDL is interactive, since the adversary queries an oracle that computes discrete logarithms, so it is not falsifiable in the sense of Naor~\cite{C:Naor03} and resists cryptanalytic validation. There are also meta-reduction barriers. Paillier and Vergnaud rule out standard-model proofs of Schnorr unforgeability from DL-type assumptions for algebraic reductions~\cite{AC:PaiVer05}, and later work rules out tight generic reductions in the ROM~\cite{C:GarBhaLok08,EC:Seurin12,JC:FleJagSch19}. For Dilithium we are not aware of any standard-model security proof. To our knowledge, no standard-model unforgeability proof for Schnorr under an honestly generated concrete hash family was previously known. Prior work gives key recovery for arbitrary hash functions~\cite{AC:PaiVer05}, necessary hash properties with generic-group sufficiency~\cite{JMC:NevSmaWar09}, and positive Fiat--Shamir instantiation for highly sound protocols under a bounded notion~\cite{DBLP:journals/tcs/MittelbachV18}.

This paper addresses the question directly. We give standard-model instantiations under non-interactive assumptions, for Schnorr and for a variant of Dilithium. We work with a keyed hash family whose key is generated honestly and published in the verification key; how our reductions pass the meta-reduction barriers is explained at the end of Section~\ref{sec-results}.

\subsection{Our Results} \label{sec-results}

For Schnorr we give one result for key recovery and two for unforgeability, and we then repeat the first two steps of this development in the lattice setting; the third is deferred, with the plan recorded at the end of Part~II. In each construction the hash function is keyed. The hash key is generated with the signing key and included in the verification key, as in the full-domain-hash instantiation of Hohenberger, Sahai, and Waters~\cite{EC:HohSahWat14}.

\heading{Key recovery from DL.} We define \emph{tunable lossy functions} (TLFs). Fixing the image parameter in a TLF gives a standard fixed-parameter lossy function, while Zhandry's extremely lossy functions (ELFs), in their strongly efficiently enumerable form, give a family of choices for that parameter. A TLF key is generated in injective or lossy mode with an image parameter $r$. In lossy mode the image can be enumerated. Theorem~\ref{thm-bk} gives a concrete bound for every $r$. The mode-switch term is the TLF distinguishing advantage, while the DL reduction runs in time proportional to the enumeration cost and to $r$ per signing query.

For the main corollary we use Zhandry's ELF cascade~\cite{C:Zhandry16}. Its injective distribution is independent of $r$, and for each fixed PPT attack the proof may choose a sufficiently large polynomial $r$. The image can then be enumerated in polynomial time. Corollary~\ref{cor-bk} therefore assumes ordinary DL in the Schnorr group, together with, when instantiated with Zhandry's construction, exponential DDH in its auxiliary groups. The construction also composes the lossy function with an outer collision-resistant hash, which maps its range into $\Zp$; collision resistance is what rules out a constant outer map, whose one-element image would let Proposition~\ref{prop-lossy-forge} forge in constant time, and the key-recovery reduction does not otherwise use it. The direct Peikert--Waters construction~\cite{STOC:PeiWat08} gives a candidate fixed-$r$ tradeoff, discussed in Section~\ref{sec-lf-thm}. Compared with Paillier and Vergnaud~\cite{AC:PaiVer05}, our result covers one particular keyed hash family rather than every hash function, but it replaces the interactive OMDL assumption by non-interactive assumptions.

The two modes of the lossy function are not a convenience. The rejection-sampling simulator uses only the verification key, so an adversary can run it too. A hash function with an image of size $r$ makes Schnorr universally forgeable in time about $r$ (Proposition~\ref{prop-lossy-forge}). The deployed scheme therefore uses the injective mode, where no such attack exists, and the lossy mode appears only inside the proof. The same proposition shows that lossiness alone cannot give unforgeability, whatever the lossy function: an image small enough to rejection-sample against is small enough to forge against. Our second result therefore uses a different tool.

\heading{Static-message unforgeability from CDL.} Our second result instantiates the hash function with an obfuscated circuit and proves \emph{static-message unforgeability}, denoted EUF-static. The forger declares its signing messages and forgery message before seeing the verification key. This is weaker than the selective-target notion of~\cite{EC:HohSahWat14}, where only the forgery message is declared in advance. Theorem~\ref{thm-uf} assumes the circular discrete-logarithm (CDL) problem of Cho, Fuchsbauer, O'Neill, and Sefranek~\cite{C:CFOS25}, indistinguishability obfuscation~\cite{C:BGIRSVY01,SIAM:BGIRSVH16,STOC:JaiLinSah21}, and puncturable PRFs~\cite{AC:BonWat13,EC:BoyGilIsh15,CCS:KPTZ13}. It requires ordinary PPT security at the level of these three primitives. Known iO constructions use stronger quantitative assumptions on their underlying primitives. A polynomial $Q$ determines the size to which the hash circuit is padded, so the construction is a family indexed by $Q$, and Corollary~\ref{cor-uf} gives security for forgers declaring at most $Q$ messages. This bounded notion, which we write $\mathrm{EUF\text{-}static}[Q]$, is weaker than ordinary static security of a single scheme, and the discussion after the corollary explains why the gap is not a notational one. Bounded notions of this shape also appear in the standard-model Fiat--Shamir instantiation of Mittelbach and Venturi~\cite{DBLP:journals/tcs/MittelbachV18}.

\heading{Adaptive security for salted Schnorr.} Our third result makes one change to Schnorr: the signer samples a uniform salt, includes it in the hash input, and transmits it with the signature. The nonce and salt are sampled uniformly and independently. Theorem~\ref{thm-adaptive} proves adaptive EUF-CMA security for every polynomial number of signing queries, without placing a query bound in the scheme. The message enters the hash through a pre-hash, a tunable lossy function in injective mode whose key is part of the verification key, and the message length is any polynomial. The proof guesses digests rather than messages: it switches the pre-hash to lossy mode and draws its guesses from the enumerated lossy image, of size at most $r$, so the guessing losses are $r$ per signing query and $r\cdot2^{\saltlen}$ for the forgery, rather than $2^{\msglen}$ and $2^{\msglen+\saltlen}$. With a quasi-polynomial $r$, quasi-polynomially secure CDL, iO, and puncturable PRFs suffice (Corollary~\ref{cor-adaptive-qp}), and the salt length is polylogarithmic.

\heading{Lattice signatures.} Our remaining results extend the program to lattice-based Fiat--Shamir with aborts, the paradigm of Lyubashevsky signatures~\cite{AC:Lyubashevsky09,EC:Lyubashevsky12} that Dilithium~\cite{Dilithium} instantiates. We treat an unstructured scheme of that shape, over a power-of-two modulus with binary-decomposition challenges. The first result is key recovery, mirroring the first Schnorr result: with the hash instantiated by a tunable lossy function, BK-CMA security holds under search LWE against time $r\cdot\mathrm{poly}$ (Theorem~\ref{thm-dil-bk}). The simulation lemma is exact, as in the group case, and no obfuscation appears. A lattice-based lossy function at a fixed image bound $r$ would make every ingredient lattice-based, at the cost of assuming search LWE against time $r\cdot\mathrm{poly}$; candidate instantiations are discussed in Section~\ref{sec-dilithium}. The second result instantiates the hash function with an obfuscated masked-decomposition circuit and proves static-message unforgeability (Theorem~\ref{thm-dil}). The circular assumption is a SIS analogue of CDL, and for this conversion function it is not an assumption at all: a two-line gadget identity converts its solutions to SIS solutions (Lemma~\ref{lem-csis-sis}). The resulting assumption profile is SIS, LWE, iO, and puncturable PRFs, all polynomially secure, at a quasi-polynomial modulus (Corollary~\ref{cor-dil-sis}); the modulus condition makes signing rejections negligibly rare, which is what lets the proof fix its programmed points in advance. A standard-model hash function also removes the QROM question that idealized-model analyses of post-quantum schemes must face. Our scheme is not Dilithium. It uses plain matrices rather than the ring structure, gadget challenges rather than the sparse set $B_\tau$, a power-of-two rather than a prime modulus, and no compression or hints. Section~\ref{sec-dilithium} states the comparison in full, and Section~\ref{sec-conclusion} records the gaps as open problems. What we claim transfers to Dilithium is the instantiation technique.

The paper is organized in two parts after the shared preliminaries. Part~I treats Schnorr signatures: key recovery (Section~\ref{sec-lf-kr}), static-message unforgeability (Section~\ref{sec-io-uf}), and adaptive security for the salted scheme (Section~\ref{sec-adaptive}, with the proof in Appendix~\ref{app-adaptive}). Part~II treats the lattice scheme: its lattice preliminaries, the scheme, and key recovery (Section~\ref{sec-dilithium}), then static-message unforgeability (Section~\ref{sec-dil-static}). Open problems for both parts are in Section~\ref{sec-conclusion}.

Our reductions are non-black-box in the hash function: they use the code of the PRF keys and, in the second result, choose the hash function's circuit. This is how the results evade the meta-reduction barriers of~\cite{AC:PaiVer05,C:GarBhaLok08,EC:Seurin12,JC:FleJagSch19}, which apply to reductions treating the assumption in a representation-independent way, and the non-programmable-ROM impossibilities of Fukumitsu and Hasegawa~\cite{FukumitsuH21}, which simulate forgers that make random-oracle queries. CDL is representation-dependent by design, and our forgers make no oracle queries.

\subsection{Technical Overview} \label{sec-techov}

\heading{The classical proof, and what breaks.} Recall Schnorr signatures over a group $\GG = \langle g \rangle$ of prime order $p$. The secret key is $x \getsr \Zp$ with public key $X = g^x$; a signature on $m$ is $(R, s)$ with $R = g^r$, $c = H(R, m)$, and $s = r + cx \bmod p$; verification checks $g^s = R \cdot X^c$. In the ROM proof, the reduction answers a signing query by sampling $s, c \getsr \Zp$, setting $R \gets g^s X^{-c}$, and \emph{programming} the random oracle so that $H(R,m) = c$. A standard-model hash function cannot be programmed, as it is fixed before the adversary runs. Our proofs instead arrange that the simulated signatures already verify under the fixed function.

\heading{Result 1: rejection sampling over a lossy image.} Let $K$ be a TLF key and $k$ an outer collision-resistant-hash key. We define
$$
H_{K,k}(R,m)=\CRHFEv\bigl(k,\TLFEv(K,\enc{R,m})\bigr).
$$
The proof switches $K$ from injective to lossy mode at a chosen image bound $r$. Let $C$ be the set obtained by applying the outer hash to the enumerated lossy image. To sign $m$ without the secret key, the reduction samples $s\getsr\Zp$ and $c\getsr C$, sets $R=g^sX^{-c}$, and accepts if $H_{K,k}(R,m)=c$.

Each trial accepts with probability exactly $1/|C|$. Conditioned on acceptance, the output is an honest signature because the good pairs $(c,s)$ correspond one-to-one with group elements $R$ through $c=H_{K,k}(R,m)$ and $s=\mathsf{dlog}_g(R)+cx$. Thus, a successful key-recovery adversary in this simulated game solves DL. Theorem~\ref{thm-bk} give the advantage and running time of the simulation. With a strongly efficiently enumerable ELF, the proof chooses polynomial $r$, so the DL reduction is PPT.

\heading{Result 2: an obfuscated hash.} For unforgeability, the hash value in a forgery must give a CDL solution. We start from the ROM proof of~\cite{C:CFOS25}. Its reduction answers a hash query $(R,m)$ with $f(Rh^ag^b)+a$ for fresh $a,b\getsr\Zp$, where $h$ is the CDL challenge and $f$ is the conversion function. These values are uniform, and the known pair $(a,b)$ converts a forgery to a CDL solution. Our hash function is an obfuscated circuit that computes the same expression, with $X$ in place of $h$ and with $a,b$ computed by puncturable PRFs on $(R,m)$.

Signing queries are handled by hardwiring. Since the messages are declared before the verification key is published, the proof samples $s_i,v_i\getsr\Zp$, sets $R_i=g^{s_i}X^{-v_i}$, and hardwires $(R_i,m_i)\mapsto v_i$. Puncturable PRFs make this circuit indistinguishable from the honest one. Before applying this change of variables, the proof replaces the honest hash value at $(R_i,m_i)$ by an independent uniform value. This replacement is exact because the two PRF masks at that point are uniform and occur nowhere else. A forgery is on a fresh message, so it is evaluated by the PRF-computed part of the circuit and gives a CDL solution. We choose $f$ to have no zeros, ensuring that the CDL game accepts the resulting solution. The hardwired entries make the proof circuit grow linearly with $Q$.

\heading{Adaptive queries and the salt.} The static proof punctures at points containing messages that must be known before the circuit is published. The salted construction removes this restriction with two devices. First, the message enters the hash through a \emph{pre-hash}, a tunable lossy function in injective mode, and everything downstream depends on the message only through its digest $d$. Second, the construction sets $u=(d,\salt)$ and uses two additional PRFs to compute $c_u,s_u\in\Zp$ and the trigger point $W_u=g^{s_u}X^{-c_u}$. On input $(R,d,\salt)$, the circuit returns $c_u$ when $R=W_u$; otherwise it evaluates the main branch. The actual signer still samples $r'\getsr\Zp$ and sets $R=g^{r'}$, exactly as in ordinary randomized Schnorr. The point $W_u$ is used by the reduction, which signs without $x$ by returning $(W_u,s_u,\salt)$. If the trigger were indexed only by the digest, then two signing queries on the same message would give the reduction the same response. A fresh salt gives each query a new pair $u$, except with negligible collision probability, and does not occur in the Schnorr verification equation.

For signing query $j$, the reduction samples $\salt_j$ and guesses $m_j$ before publishing the circuit. Set $u_j=(m_j,\salt_j)$. The hybrid punctures $\PRF_3,\PRF_4$ at $\enczero{u_j}$ and punctures $\PRF_1,\PRF_2$ at $\encone{R_j,u_j}$ and $\encone{W_j,u_j}$, where $R_j$ is the honest signature point. After replacing the punctured values by uniform values and changing variables, the two triples are
$$
(R_j,v_j,s_{j,0})=(g^{s_{j,0}}X^{-v_j},v_j,s_{j,0})
\quad\text{and}\quad
(W_j,c_j,s_{j,1})=(g^{s_{j,1}}X^{-c_j},c_j,s_{j,1}),
$$
where $v_j,s_{j,0},c_j,s_{j,1}$ are independent and uniform. Conditional on $R_j\ne W_j$, the triples are identically distributed. The temporary circuit stores the two point-value pairs in a canonical order and does not record which one came from honest signing. Exchanging the returned response is therefore exact. The proof then reverses the preceding changes, leaving query $j$ answered at the trigger point.

A forgery that takes the main branch gives a CDL solution as in the static proof. For a forgery that takes the trigger branch, the reduction guesses $u^*=(m^*,\salt^*)$, punctures $\PRF_4$ at $\enczero{u^*}$, and replaces $g^{\PRFEv_4(k_4,\enczero{u^*})}$ by a DL challenge. A valid response reveals its discrete logarithm. The guesses of the digests of the queried messages in the signing hybrids and of $u^*$ in the forgery reduction give the quasi-polynomial losses in Theorem~\ref{thm-adaptive}; both are drawn from the enumerated lossy image of the pre-hash, which is why they cost $r$ rather than $2^{\msglen}$. A proof that could puncture at these adaptively selected inputs, even given the published obfuscation, would remove the guessing entirely. Unsalted adaptive security without a query bound remains open.

\subsection{Related Work} \label{sec-relwork}

\heading{Schnorr in idealized models.} Pointcheval and Stern~\cite{EC:PoiSte96,JC:PoiSte00} proved EUF-CMA security of Schnorr signatures in the ROM under DL, with a loose reduction. Tight reductions are known in the ROM combined with the algebraic group model~\cite{EC:FucPloSeu20} and in generic group models~\cite{JMC:NevSmaWar09,EPRINT:Shoup23,C:CLMQ21}. In the ROM alone, reductions are known under the interactive MBDL assumption~\cite{INDOCRYPT:BelDai20} and, with some loss, under DDH-type assumptions~\cite{C:RotSeg21}.

CFOS~\cite{C:CFOS25} give a completely tight ROM reduction under CDL, a non-interactive and falsifiable assumption. Our second result builds directly on their simulation strategy.

\heading{Barriers.} Meta-reductions rule out tight generic reductions for Schnorr in the ROM~\cite{AC:PaiVer05,C:GarBhaLok08,EC:Seurin12,JC:FleJagSch19}, and rule out standard-model proofs in the non-programmable ROM for certain reduction classes~\cite{FukumitsuH21}. Both classes cover reductions that treat the assumption in a representation-independent way and run the forger as a black box. Our reductions do neither, as explained at the end of Section~\ref{sec-results}. Mittelbach and Venturi~\cite{DBLP:journals/tcs/MittelbachV18} give positive standard-model Fiat--Shamir results for highly sound protocols, using obfuscation and puncturable PRFs and a query-bounded security notion; their setting is the soundness one described above, and the bounded notion is the same shape as our Corollary~\ref{cor-uf}. Neven, Smart, and Warinschi~\cite{JMC:NevSmaWar09} identify standard-model properties that a Schnorr hash function must have, such as random-prefix preimage resistance, and show sufficiency in the generic group model; they do not give a standard-model unforgeability proof. The uninstantiability results for Fiat--Shamir~\cite{FOCS:GolKal03}, and for random-oracle schemes generally~\cite{JACM:CanGolHal04}, use contrived constructions, and the correlation-intractability line~\cite{EC:CCRR18,STOC:CCHLRRW19,C:PeiShi19} covers statistically sound proofs; Section~\ref{sec-intro} explains why neither reaches the schemes treated here.

\heading{Instantiating random oracles.} Hohenberger, Sahai, and Waters~\cite{EC:HohSahWat14} instantiate full-domain hash for RSA and BLS signatures using iO and puncturable PRFs. Their selective result fixes only the forgery message before key generation and allows adaptive signing queries, whereas our static result fixes the signing messages as well. Both proofs puncture PRFs, but their signing simulations differ. There are two further differences. For Schnorr, the signer chooses the group element $R$ in each signature, and the proof uses this freedom by choosing those points in advance and hardwiring their hash values, in the manner of honest-verifier zero-knowledge simulation. Also, the forgery is converted to a CDL solution rather than inverted using the trapdoor structure of RSA/BLS. Lossy functions were introduced by Peikert and Waters~\cite{STOC:PeiWat08,JACM:PeiWat11}. Zhandry~\cite{C:Zhandry16} introduced their extremely lossy variant and used it to instantiate several random-oracle properties; Fischlin and Rohrbach~\cite{TCC:FisRoh23} showed ELFs cannot be built from a random oracle. Murphy, O'Neill, and Zaheri~\cite{AC:MurONeZah22} gave instantiation results for OAEP-type and Fujisaki--Okamoto-type transforms using obfuscation-based tools. We are not aware of prior standard-model instantiations of Schnorr signatures beyond~\cite{AC:PaiVer05}.

\heading{Keyed hashing in the verification key.} In all three constructions the hash function used for the instantiation is keyed. Moreover, in our two unforgeability results the hash key depends on the public key of the signer. This is undesirable in that we would like to show and independent hash key works, which is more consistent with cryptographic practice (e.g., SHA is not designed with Schnorr in mind). However, at present this seems technically difficult.

Nevertheless, we stress that such instantiations \emph{still refute uninstantiability}. For example the CGH theorem is stated for an independently sampled hash key, but the proof extends to public-key-dependent or jointly generated instantiations, since the concrete attack uses only public evaluation of the hash function, not independence from other keys of the overlying scheme. Moreover, such a restriction was also present in prior instantiability work~\cite{EC:HohSahWat14}.
