\section{Overview}
Proofs in the \emph{random oracle model} (ROM) underpin many deployed cryptographic
schemes---from TLS and Bitcoin to NIST post-quantum candidates---yet their practical
security guarantees remain unclear. This project develops a principled framework to
increase confidence in ROM proofs, comprising two complementary thrusts:
\begin{newenum}
\item \emph{ROM tightness}, ensuring 
proof-based parameter choices at standard security levels remain meaningful in practice; and
\item \emph{Instantiability}, ensuring random oracles can be safely replaced with
real hash functions.
\end{newenum}
Building on our circular-discrete-logarithm (CDL) assumption introduced at
CRYPTO~2025, we propose and analyze the \emph{Vandermonde-CDL} and \emph{Circular Module
Short-Integer-Solution} assumptions. These yield the \textbf{first tight ROM proofs} not
only for Schnorr signatures (as in CRYPTO 2025) but also for emerging extensions such as
threshold Schnorr signatures ($\mathsf{Sparkle+}$), $\mathsf{Dilithium}$, and range proofs
based on $\mathsf{Bulletproofs}$, all of which are seeing standardization and deployment.
All new assumptions remain amenable to cryptanalytic validation. In parallel, new
cryptographic tools based on extremely lossy functions (ELFs) and related notions will
provide the \textbf{first standard-model instantiations} of Schnorr signatures and the
Fujisaki--Okamoto ($\mathsf{FO}$) transform.

\noindent\textbf{Keywords}: Cryptography; random oracle model; digital signatures;
public-key encryption.

\section{Intellectual Merit}
The project addresses fundamental open problems in provable security with direct
implications for practice. Planned contributions include:
\begin{newitemize}
  \item  \textbf{Tight ROM reductions} for  threshold Schnorr under an assumption we call Vandermonde-CDL.
  \item The first \textbf{standard-model instantiations} of Schnorr signatures and the Fujisaki-Okamoto transform 
        using new ELF relaxations that capture plausible properties of cryptographic hash functions.
  \item \textbf{New tools} --- \emph{tunable} lossy functions, \emph{adaptive-leakage} universal computational extractors,
        and public-key encryption secure against \emph{functional-plaintext-checking attack} --- that
        expand our toolbox for bridging ROM analyses with real-world security.
\end{newitemize}
In summary, in advancing approachable assumptions to address long-standing questions, the project
will push the boundaries of provable security for standardized and widely-deployed
schemes.

\section{Broader Impacts}The project is uniquely positioned to shape   standards: 
as a former NIST visiting faculty member, the PI has established connections, 
and the project’s questions align directly with NIST’s post-quantum and threshold 
priorities. Outcomes will deliver timely, research-driven input to NIST workshops. 
At UMass, the PI will promote early research exposure through (i) a new 
cryptography subgroup in the 250+ member \emph{Cybersecurity Club}, 
(ii) engagement of first- and second-year undergraduates in the 
\emph{Early Research Scholars Program}, and 
(iii) community discussions on privacy through the 
\emph{Office of Community, Outreach, and Organizational Learning}. 
Planned outcomes include mentoring one postdoc, one PhD, two MS, and four 
undergraduates, sustained outreach across educational levels, 
and tangible contributions to NIST and industry standards.
